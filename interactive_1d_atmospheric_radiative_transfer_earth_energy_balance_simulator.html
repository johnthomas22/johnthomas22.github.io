Page 1: Mathematical Proofs & Model Verification

The mathematical validity of the simulation can be verified directly using closed-form analytical solutions of the governing differential equations.

Proof 1: Equilibrium Climate Sensitivity (ECS) Closed-Form Limit

At thermal equilibrium, time derivatives vanish (dΔTs/dt = 0, dΔTd/dt = 0). Consequently, the mixed layer and deep ocean equilibrate to the exact same temperature (ΔTs = ΔTd = ΔTeq). Substituting this into the mixed-layer ordinary differential equation:

Cm · (dΔTs / dt) = ΔF - λ·ΔTs - γ·(ΔTs - ΔTd) = 0
ΔF - λ·ΔTeq - γ·(ΔTeq - ΔTeq) = 0
ΔF - λ·ΔTeq = 0 ⟹ ΔTeq = ΔF / λ

For a doubling of atmospheric CO₂ (C / C0 = 2), the effective radiative forcing (IPCC AR6 Working Group I, Chapter 7) is ΔF2× = 3.93 W/m² (giving α = 3.93 / ln(2) ≈ 5.670 W/m²). With Equilibrium Climate Sensitivity ECS = 3.00 °C, the climate feedback parameter is derived from F2× / ECS as λ = 3.93 / 3.00 = 1.310 W/m²/K:

ΔTeq = 3.930 W/m² / 1.310 W/m²/K = 3.000 °C

Verification: Clicking "3. Instant 2× CO₂ Pulse" and then "⚡ Instant Eq." sets ΔT to exactly +3.00 °C and Net Imbalance (EEI) = 0.00 W/m².

Proof 2: Analytical Derivation of Effective Radiating Altitude (zeff in Metres)

Earth's Outgoing Longwave Radiation (OLR) escaping to space originates from the altitude where the atmosphere becomes optically thin. Inverting the Stefan-Boltzmann law:

OLR = σ · (Teff)⁴ ⟹ Teff = (OLR / σ)1/4

Coupling this with the standard tropospheric lapse rate (Γ = 0.0065 K/m, or 6.5 °C/km), where T(z) = Ts - Γ·z:

Teff = Ts - Γ · zeff ⟹ zeff (metres) = (Ts - Teff) / 0.0065

At pre-industrial equilibrium (OLR = 239.0 W/m², Ts = 13.85 °C = 287.00 K, σ = 5.670374 × 10⁻⁸ W/m²/K⁴):

Teff = (239.0 / 5.670374×10⁻⁸)1/4 = 254.80 K (-18.35 °C)
ΔT = 287.00 K - 254.80 K = 32.20 K
zeff = 32.20 K / 0.0065 K/m = 4,953.8 metres (displayed as 4,954 m)

Verification: In the pre-industrial baseline state, the app displays 4,954 m (-18.4 °C), matching the analytical calculation.

Proof 3: Eigenmode Decomposition of Ocean Thermal Lag

The two-layer ocean differential system exhibits two distinct physical eigenvalues corresponding to the fast upper-ocean response and slow deep-ocean heat uptake:

τfast ≈ 3.6 years (Upper ocean mixed layer warming)
τslow ≈ 230 years (Deep ocean thermal diffusion)

Because human emissions ramp up year after year (+2.5 ppm/yr), surface warming continuously lags radiative forcing, sustaining a modeled Earth Energy Imbalance of ≈ +0.78 W/m² (consistent with modern satellite- and Argo-observed estimates of ~0.8 to 1.0+ W/m²).

Historical Hindcast & The Baseline Question: Why 1750 vs. 1850–1900?

A common question when interpreting climate models is why the differential equations integrate from 1750 CE when virtually all public targets (the UNFCCC Paris Agreement 1.5 °C / 2.0 °C goals, IPCC policy summaries, and tipping-point literature) are referenced to the 1850–1900 baseline:

🌍 1750: The Physical Equilibrium Baseline
Why climate physics starts in 1750: The fundamental greenhouse forcing equation ΔF = α·ln(C / C₀) and the two-layer differential equations require an unperturbed thermodynamic rest state. In 1750, atmospheric CO₂ was stable at ~278–280 ppm (from Antarctic ice cores), ocean heat uptake was in balance, and the planetary energy imbalance was zero (EEI = 0, ΔTs = 0, ΔTd = 0). Starting here avoids having to guess arbitrary non-zero initial ocean heat fluxes.
🌡️ 1850–1900: The Observational Thermometer Baseline
Why public policy starts in 1850–1900: Reliable worldwide instrumental thermometer networks (HadCRUT, Berkeley Earth, NOAA) only began around 1850. Because global surface thermometers did not exist in 1750, the IPCC and Paris Agreement operationally defined "pre-industrial" as the 1850–1900 multi-decadal observational mean.

Bridging the ~0.12 °C Early-Industrial Offset: Between 1750 and the late 19th century, early coal burning and agricultural land clearance nudged atmospheric CO₂ from 280 ppm to ~291 ppm, warming the planet slightly before global thermometer networks existed. In IPCC AR6 Working Group I (Chapter 2), paleoclimate reconstructions estimate this early warming at ~0.1 °C (likely range -0.05 °C to +0.23 °C).

When our two-box EBM hindcast runs forward from 1750 through historical Law Dome and Mauna Loa Keeling data, it calculates an average 1850–1900 surface warming of exactly +0.121 °C above 1750 (in near-perfect agreement with the IPCC central estimate). The simulator harmonizes both frameworks:

  • Physical Radiative & Ocean Integration: Solved forward from the unforced 1750 equilibrium (ΔT = 0, EEI = 0) with zero artificial initialization fudge factors.
  • Tipping Points & Chart Reference Lines: Evaluated relative to the standard 1850–1900 Paris baseline by accounting for the model's +0.12 °C offset, ensuring all threshold crossing dates (e.g. Greenland Ice Sheet, Coral Reefs) align with published empirical science (Armstrong McKay et al. 2022).

2. Polar Amplification & Global Mean Surface Temperature (GMST) Scaling

Antarctic ice-core deuterium isotopes (δD in Jouzel et al. 2007) record local East Antarctic site temperature swings of ~10 °C to 12 °C between deep glacial minimums and interglacial peaks. Due to polar amplification, polar temperature changes are roughly ~2× larger than the global average. Following Tierney et al. (2020), Shakun et al. (2012), and IPCC AR6 Chapter 2, Antarctic isotope anomalies are scaled by the empirical global-to-polar ratio (~0.52) so that the Last Glacial Maximum (21,000 BP) corresponds to a Global Mean Surface Temperature anomaly of -6.0 °C relative to 1750 CE, and warm interglacials (such as the Eemian / MIS 5e at 125,000 BP) peak at +1.0 °C to +1.4 °C.

3. Resolution Limits & Why the 500-Year Window Comparison Is Robust

• Fastest Prehistoric Global Warming (Termination I, ~15,000–14,500 BP): +0.35 °C per 500 yrs (+0.070 °C / century)
• Typical Holocene Natural Variability (6,000 BP): ±0.04 °C per 500 yrs (±0.008 °C / century)
• Observed & Projected Modern Trajectory (1958–2058 CE at +2.5 ppm/yr): +2.15 °C per 100 yrs (~31× faster than Termination I)

In deep ice cores (400,000 to 800,000 BP) and marine sediment cores (800,000 to 1,000,000 BP), molecular diffusion and core compression smooth out sub-centennial fluctuations. However, in the younger record (0 to 50,000 BP)—where WAIS Divide, NGRIP, and global deglacial stacks preserve 20-to-100-year resolution—we can directly resolve the fastest natural global warming events in Earth's recent history (Orbital Glacial Terminations). Even during the steepest 500-year phase of Termination I (15,000 to 14,500 BP), global temperature rose by ~0.35 °C over five centuries, driven by orbital Milankovitch insolation shifts and an ~8 ppm natural CO₂ outgassing from warming oceans. By contrast, human industrial emissions have added +149 ppm of CO₂ since 1750 (and over +110 ppm since 1958 alone), driving a warming slope approximately 31 times steeper than peak natural deglaciation.

Addendum: Present-Day Forced Warming (+1.26 °C) vs. Headlines Reporting +1.5 °C Breached

A common point of inquiry when reviewing climate models is: "Why does this physical simulator model a warming anomaly of approximately +1.26 °C for 2026, when international headlines in 2023–2024 widely reported that global temperatures had already breached +1.5 °C?"

This apparent divergence is grounded in the critical distinction between single-year weather variability (driven by chaotic internal ocean oscillations like El Niño) and the underlying multi-decadal forced climate response formally defined by the IPCC and the United Nations Paris Agreement.

1. Single-Year Anomaly Spikes vs. The Multi-Decadal Forced Trend

During calendar years 2023 and 2024, observational monitoring agencies (Copernicus / ECMWF ERA5, Berkeley Earth, NOAA GlobalTemp, NASA GISTEMP) reported that global surface air temperatures temporarily reached +1.48 °C to +1.55 °C above the 1850–1900 reference level.

However, these single-year records were amplified by a temporary confluence of natural internal variability stacked on top of human global warming:

  • A Powerful El Niño (ENSO): El Niño events temporarily vent vast quantities of ocean heat from the tropical Pacific into the troposphere, spiking global mean surface temperatures by +0.10 °C to +0.25 °C above the forced baseline. As El Niño decays into neutral or La Niña conditions, global temperatures naturally drop back toward the forced trend line.
  • Solar Cycle 25 Maximum: The 11-year solar cycle reached its peak in 2024–2025, temporarily adding ~0.05 W/m² of top-of-atmosphere solar irradiance.
  • Marine Aerosol Reductions & Volcanic Forcing: New international shipping fuel rules (IMO 2020) reduced sulfur emissions, temporarily thinning reflective marine stratocumulus clouds over shipping corridors, while the massive January 2022 eruption of the Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai submarine volcano injected unprecedented water vapor directly into the stratosphere.

2. The Official UNFCCC & IPCC Definition of "Breaching 1.5 °C"

Under the Paris Agreement, the World Meteorological Organization (WMO), and IPCC AR6, a long-term climate threshold is not considered "breached" by a single unusually warm year or month. Climate is scientifically defined as the 20-year to 30-year running average of global temperatures, which filters out short-term ENSO noise:

• Single-Year Observational Spikes (2023–2024 El Niño Peak): +1.48 °C to +1.55 °C (relative to 1850–1900; temporary weather record)
• Human-Induced Forced Warming (Forster et al. 2024, ESSD): +1.31 °C [1.1 to 1.7 °C] in 2023, ~1.36 °C in 2024 (relative to 1850–1900)
• Simulator Model State (2026 Forced Anomaly): +1.26 °C rel. to 1750 (+1.14 °C rel. to 1850–1900)
• Projected Permanent Crossing of the 20-Year Mean (+1.5 °C): Early-to-mid 2030s (under continued emissions)

The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6 Working Group I) and the authoritative Indicators of Global Climate Change 2023 (Forster et al. 2024, ESSD) define human-induced warming as the multi-decadal forced response to greenhouse gases and aerosols relative to the 1850–1900 reference period, estimated at +1.31 °C [1.1 to 1.7 °C] in 2023 and reaching approximately +1.36 °C in 2024. Relative to the unforced pre-industrial 1750 baseline (which includes ~0.12 °C of early warming between 1750 and 1900), the simulator's deterministic forced response reaches +1.26 °C in 2026 (equivalent to +1.14 °C above the 1850–1900 mean).

3. Pre-Industrial Baseline Differences: 1750 vs. 1850–1900

Another source of numerical variation in the literature is the choice of pre-industrial baseline:

  • The 1850–1900 Meteorological Baseline: Observational weather datasets (HadCRUT5, Berkeley Earth) use 1850–1900 because that was the earliest era with standardized, globally distributed thermometer networks.
  • The 1750 Pre-Industrial Baseline: In physical radiative transfer models (and Antarctic ice cores), the true pre-industrial baseline is 1750 CE, when atmospheric CO₂ was in steady-state equilibrium at 278 ppm prior to industrial coal combustion.

Between 1750 and 1880, early industrial coal burning and land-clearing had already elevated CO₂ from 278 ppm to ~290 ppm, producing approximately +0.12 °C of early warming before global thermometer networks were established. Consequently, tipping-point thresholds (Armstrong McKay et al. 2022) and Paris Agreement targets are referenced to 1850–1900, while physical radiative balance simulations are referenced to the 1750 unforced baseline. The simulator accounts for this by computing the model's 1850–1900 hindcast offset (mean +0.12 °C) and adjusting threshold crossings accordingly.

4. Why the Two-Layer Ocean EBM Models the Forced Signal

This simulator models the foundational physics of planetary energy balance and the Two-Layer Ocean Energy Balance Model (Held et al. 2010; Geoffroy et al. 2013; IPCC AR6 Chapter 7). Because it computes the deterministic thermal response to greenhouse gas forcing without synthesizing stochastic weather noise (like individual El Niño cycles or volcanic eruptions), it calculates the pure forced thermodynamic climate response.

At 427 ppm CO₂ in 2026, the model computes an Earth Energy Imbalance of ≈ +0.78 W/m² being absorbed by the ocean thermal sink (observational satellite & Argo ocean heat content estimates typically report ~0.8 to 1.0+ W/m² due to internal weather variability and aerosol changes), and the model's transient surface temperature anomaly lands precisely at +1.26 °C above the 1750 baseline (+1.14 °C above 1850–1900).
