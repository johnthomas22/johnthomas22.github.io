Home Decarbonisation: Insulation, Heat Pumps & Induction Cooking

Space heating accounts for over 60% of British household energy consumption

In the United Kingdom, domestic buildings are responsible for roughly 17% of total greenhouse gas emissions, primarily from burning fossil natural gas in over 24 million boilers. Decarbonising a home follows a strict physical hierarchy:

1. Fabric First: Insulation

Loft insulation (to 270–300mm), cavity wall insulation, and draught-proofing reduce heat loss by 30% to 50%. This lowers the heat output required to keep rooms at 20°C, meaning a replacement heat pump can run at low flow temperatures (35°C–45°C), where its thermodynamic efficiency is highest.

2. Heat Pumps (COP 3.0 to 4.0)

Gas boilers burn methane at ~85%–92% efficiency. A heat pump does not manufacture heat from fuel; it uses a closed refrigeration cycle to extract and amplify ambient environmental heat from outside air or the ground. For every 1.0 kWh of electricity consumed, it delivers 3.0 to 4.0 kWh of heat into your home. On Great Britain's coal-free grid, this slashes heating carbon by >80%.

3. Induction Cooking & Clean Air

Gas hobs vent nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), carbon monoxide, benzene, and particulate matter directly into kitchen air, elevating childhood asthma risks by ~42%. Induction hobs use electromagnetic coils to heat the iron base of cookware directly with 85%–90% thermal efficiency (vs ~35% for an open gas flame), eliminating indoor fossil fuel combustion entirely.

Transport: Electric Vehicles, Rail, and Compassionate Realism on Flights

Surface transport is the single largest greenhouse gas emitting sector in the UK (~24%)

The physics of moving human beings and cargo is dictated by propulsion efficiency and rolling resistance:

  • Electric Vehicles vs. Internal Combustion Engines (ICE): A petrol engine is essentially a rolling space heater; roughly 70% to 75% of the fuel's chemical energy is wasted as friction and hot exhaust gas. An electric motor converts over 90% of battery energy into wheel torque. Even when accounting for battery manufacturing in overseas factories, life-cycle studies confirm an EV in the UK reaches full carbon parity within ~12,000 miles of driving and produces ~70% fewer lifetime emissions.
  • Trains and Buses (Modal Shift): Steel wheels on steel rails reduce rolling friction by an order of magnitude. An electrified train journey produces ~15 to 30 g CO₂e per passenger-kilometre, compared to ~170 to 210 g for a solo petrol car driver.
  • Aviation & High-Altitude Contrail Warming: Burning kerosene at 35,000 feet causes disproportionate radiative forcing. In addition to CO₂, jet engines emit nitrogen oxides (which alter ozone and methane) and water vapour that forms linear cirrus contrails, trapping outgoing thermal infrared radiation. Aviation's total warming impact is approximately 1.7× to 2.0× its pure CO₂ emissions.
  • The Realistic Compassion Boundary (Long-Haul Family Ties): While short-haul domestic flights (e.g. London to Edinburgh or Manchester) can readily be replaced by electric rail, intercontinental travel has no current low-carbon alternative. If your family lives in Melbourne, Auckland, Toronto, or Mumbai, nobody expects you to spend six weeks on a cargo vessel or sever human bonds. Eliminating routine regional flights, driving an EV or cycling locally, and reducing beef consumption easily compensates for an occasional family reunion flight.

Food: Buying Local, Eating Less Meat & The 10,000-Mile Fruit Reality

Why what you eat matters 10× to 50× more than where it was grown

A common intuition is that eating local food is the best way to cut carbon. While supporting local farmers is fantastic for community resilience and fresh produce, food transport accounts for only ~5% to 6% of food's total carbon footprint (Poore & Nemecek, Science 2018; Our World in Data). The emissions released during food production—enteric methane, deforestation, fertilisers, and manure—dominate by an order of magnitude.

🔬 The 10,000-Mile Fruit vs. Local Farm Beef Fact:

Even if a punnet of fresh berries or mangoes is flown 10,000 miles by cargo aeroplane, its carbon footprint (~15 to 20 kg CO₂e per kg) is far lower than beef produced on a British farm right next door (60 to 100 kg CO₂e per kg)!

And if fruit travels 10,000 miles by refrigerated container sea freight (such as bananas or apples from South America), shipping emissions are tiny (~0.1 to 0.3 kg CO₂e/kg), meaning its total carbon footprint is less than 1% that of local beef.

Why is Meat so Carbon-Intensive?

  • Trophic Energy Losses (10:1 conversion): Animals burn calories moving, breathing, and maintaining body heat. It takes ~7 to 10 kg of plant grains or forage to produce 1 kg of beef. Eating plant proteins directly cuts out the 90% thermodynamic middleman.
  • Enteric Fermentation (Methane): Cattle and sheep are ruminants. Microbes in their rumen break down cellulose, emitting methane (CH₄)—a greenhouse gas with 84× the warming potency of CO₂ over a 20-year timescale.
  • Land Use Opportunity Cost: Livestock uses 77% of all agricultural land worldwide, but produces only 18% of global calories and 37% of protein. Swapping to plant-rich meals frees up vast land areas for peatland rewetting, commercial timber, and woodland carbon capture.
