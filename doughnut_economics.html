🧭 The 7 Ways to Think Like a 21st-Century Economist (Kate Raworth)

Mainstream economics was conceived for an empty world in the 18th and 19th centuries. Surviving the 21st century requires replacing outdated axioms with complex systems thinking.

Way 1
Change the Goal
Old: Perpetual GDP Growth
New: Thriving in Balance in the Doughnut
GDP only measures monetary flow, treating pollution cleanup and disaster recovery as positive progress. The true goal must be human well-being within ecological limits.
Way 2
See the Big Picture
Old: Self-Contained Circular Market
New: Embedded Economy
The economy is not autonomous: it is embedded within society, which is entirely nested inside the biosphere and powered by solar radiation. The household and commons are foundational.
Way 3
Nurture Human Nature
Old: Rational Economic Man (Homo economicus)
New: Social, Adaptable Humans
Human beings are not self-interested, atomized calculators. We are deeply social, interdependent, reciprocal, values-driven, and intrinsically motivated to care for our communities.
Way 4
Get Savvy with Systems
Old: Mechanical Market Equilibrium
New: Dynamic Complex Systems
Supply and demand curves do not smoothly balance. The real economy is governed by reinforcing feedbacks, delayed ocean lags, non-linear tipping cascades, and emergent phenomena.
Way 5
Design to Distribute
Old: "Growth will trickle down eventually"
New: Distributive by Design
The Kuznets curve was an empirical illusion. Wealth automatically concentrates unless institutions (land ownership, patent rights, money creation, and corporate equity) are deliberately shared.
Way 6
Create to Regenerate
Old: "Growth cleans up after itself"
New: Regenerative by Design
The environmental Kuznets curve is refuted by global supply chains. Economies must shift from linear "take-make-use-lose" pipelines to circular loops where waste equals food.
Way 7
Be Agnostic About Growth
Old: Addicted to Growth at all costs
New: Thriving whether GDP grows or not
Today we have economies that need to grow whether or not they make us thrive. In the 21st century, we need economies that make us thrive whether or not they grow.
🔬 The 9 Planetary Boundaries (Richardson et al. Science 2023 / Rockström et al.)

The ecological ceiling of the Doughnut is directly defined by peer-reviewed Earth System science. As of 2023–2026, 6 of the 9 boundaries have been transgressed due to global industrial throughput.

Boundary Dimension Control Variable Safe Boundary Threshold Current Observed Value (2026) Status
1. Climate Change Atmospheric CO₂ & Energy Imbalance 350 ppm / +1.0 W/m² 427 ppm / +2.91 W/m² Transgressed
2. Biosphere Integrity Extinction rate (E/MSY) / Biodiversity intactness < 10 E/MSY / > 90% intactness > 100–1,000 E/MSY / ~69% intactness High Risk Transgressed
3. Land-System Change Global forested land remaining vs prehistoric > 75% original forest area ~60% global forest area remaining Transgressed
4. Freshwater Change Blue water (river flow) & Green water (soil moisture) < 10.2% monthly streamflow disturbance 18.2% global land area disturbed Transgressed
5. Biogeochemical Flows Phosphorus (P) to oceans & Nitrogen (N) fixed P: 11 Mt/yr · N: 62 Mt/yr P: 22 Mt/yr · N: 150 Mt/yr (3× limit) High Risk Transgressed
6. Novel Entities Synthetic chemicals, plastics, pesticides, PFAS 0% unchecked release / full testing Hundreds of thousands untested, ubiquitous microplastics High Risk Transgressed
7. Ocean Acidification Surface seawater aragonite saturation (Ωarag) ≥ 80% of pre-industrial saturation ~81–84% (rapidly deteriorating with CO₂) Approaching Boundary
8. Atmospheric Aerosol Loading Aerosol Optical Depth (AOD) interhemispheric gap ΔAOD ≤ 0.10 Regional severe overshoot (South Asia / East Asia) Regional Transgression
9. Stratospheric Ozone Column ozone concentration (Dobson Units) ≥ 276 DU (within 5% of 290 DU baseline) ~285 DU (Recovering via 1987 Montreal Protocol) Safe (Policy Success)
